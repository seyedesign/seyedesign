Intro · How I learn

A wall full of vocabulary.

I learn English through things I enjoy: sitcoms, YouTube videos, books, and conversations. Whenever I find an interesting expression, I write it down and stick it on my wall.

The current way

Watch
Spot a line
Write it down

Then what?

Eventually, I had a wall full of vocabulary. But I couldn’t tell which expressions I’d learned, which ones needed review, or whether I could actually use them in conversation.

Learned it, or not?No sense of progress
Needs review?Nothing brings a note back

Key insight

I had built a system for collecting words. Not for learning them.

02 · First experiment

My first experiment with AI didn’t solve the problem.

I started by asking Claude to extract vocabulary from subtitle screenshots. It worked, but it returned 8–12 expressions from a single picture. I was recreating my physical wall, just digitally. So I experimented with the instructions.

Round 1

8–12 per photoSome too basic, others not very useful. Reviewing it all was overwhelming.

Round 2

Max 3 per photoBetter for subtitles, but it didn’t work for longer book pages.

Round 3

Max 1 per sentenceAbove B1 only, no duplicates, and zero results allowed when nothing is worth saving.
My feedback“You are adding a lot from only one picture… reviewing them is becoming so hard for me.”
See the new extraction rules5 rules
  1. Only above B1, common in everyday speech, and reusable.
  2. Never two items about the same idea.
  3. Skip names, rare or literary words, literal combinations.
  4. Grammar only when clearly shown, max one per input.
  5. Never repeat what’s already saved.

I didn’t need AI to find everything. I needed it to decide what was worth my attention, within rules I defined.

What I asked for, and what changed

  1. Swipe right if I know it, left if I don’t, like a dating app.

    Review became a swipe deck with spaced repetition.

  2. Today can be a bit different… a really simple calendar.

    A Today panel and a week calendar of what I added each day.

  3. Let me write or say my own example, and give me feedback.

    “Try my own example” with a verdict and a better version.

  4. The structure they use is kind of important.

    Grammar cards from the same photos.

  5. Make a short story where all today’s vocabulary is used.

    Story, situation and conversation modes.

  6. These make me speak more like native speakers.

    The Expressions tab, filtered by situation and frequency.

  7. I want to share the app with my friends.

    A Save button that shares my list, while each friend keeps their own progress.

A request I let go of

I asked for a username-and-password login. Claude pointed out that a password stored in a shared page wouldn’t protect anything, so the app syncs through my Claude account instead and shows “Signed in · synced across your devices”.

A design-system Skill

To avoid repeatedly correcting the same visual inconsistencies, I created a reusable design-system Skill with tokens, components and UI rules that Claude could follow.

The rules in practice

Lilac means “yours, today”Today panel, highlighted story phrases, the active tab
One primary actionStart review, Check my sentence, Take a photo
Colour only for resultsKnow / Not yet stamps, Correct / Almost / Not quite feedback
Reuse before addingNew patterns, like the frequency dots, are built from existing tokens

Why a skill, not a prompt

A prompt shapes one screen. A skill shapes every screen after it.

AI helped me build faster. My responsibility was to decide what belonged in the product, and whether the output solved the problem.

What I haven’t proven yet

It addresses several frustrations with my paper-based workflow, but I haven’t yet established whether it meaningfully improves:

Long-term retention

Do the expressions stay with me weeks later?

Spontaneous speaking

Do they show up when I talk, without planning?

That’s the next thing I’d want to test.

My biggest takeaway

AI is most useful when I understand the problem well enough to direct it, challenge its output, and recognise when less is actually better.
